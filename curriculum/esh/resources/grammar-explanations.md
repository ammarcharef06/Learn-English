# Grammar explanations and practice

This is a selected teaching reference, not an exhaustive grammar. Examples are original. `Base` means the infinitive without `to` (for example, `go`).

## PRE-A0 — letter names and sounds

A letter has a name and can represent different sounds in words. In `map`, the letter `a` represents a short vowel sound; in `name`, it represents a different vowel sound. Teach sound patterns in words rather than promising a fixed one-letter/one-sound rule. For this stage, focus on initial/final sounds and simple CVC blending.

**Practice:** identify the first sound in `sun`, `book`, `fish`. **Key:** /s/, /b/, /f/. Accept a clearly produced target sound; do not require IPA knowledge from the learner.

## A0 — `be` and simple present questions

Use `be` for identity, state, age, and location: `I am ready.`, `She is 20.`, `They are at home.` Make a `be` question by placing the verb before the subject: `Are they at home?` Negate by adding `not`: `She is not tired.`

## A1 — present simple and present continuous

Use the present simple for habits, repeated events, and general facts: `Mina walks to work every day.` For he/she/it, most verbs take `-s` or `-es`. Use do-support for questions and negatives: `Does Mina walk?`, `Mina does not walk.` The main verb has no third-person ending after `does`.

## A2 — past simple, `going to`, and present perfect

Use past simple for a finished past event, often with a finished-time expression: `We visited on Saturday.` Regular verbs often take `-ed`; irregular forms must be learned (`go` → `went`). Questions and negatives use `did` + base verb: `Did you go?`, `We did not go.` Not `Did you went?`

## B1 — first and second conditional; relative clauses

For a real or possible future condition, a common pattern is `if + present, will + base`: `If the buses run more often, fewer people will drive.` Do not normally put `will` in the if-clause in this pattern.

### B1 — narrative time, passive, and reported information

Use the past continuous for an action in progress when another event happened: `I was checking the list when the bus arrived.` Use past perfect only to identify an event earlier than another past reference point: `The bus had left before we reached the stop.` The past perfect is not a general marker for every past event.

## B2 — hedging, concession, and evidence

Hedging calibrates how strongly a claim is stated: `The results suggest a link` is more cautious than `The results prove a link`. Choose a hedge that reflects the evidence; hedging is not a way to hide an unsupported claim.

Use concession to acknowledge relevant opposing information: `Although the pilot was short, it identified a practical risk.` `However` commonly links contrasting clauses or sentences and needs punctuation appropriate to its position: `The pilot was short; however, it identified a risk.` Do not insert `however` as a conjunction in place of `but` without punctuation.

### B2 — probability, obligation, and conditional recommendation

`May`, `might`, and `could` can present a possibility, with strength depending on context; they do not carry a fixed percentage. `The pilot may help` is not a guarantee. For advice or obligation, `should` commonly recommends, while `must` and `have to` can express stronger necessity; the source of the obligation and context matter. Do not silently change a recommendation into a requirement.

Use a conditional when a recommendation depends on an explicit condition: `If the results remain similar in a larger sample, the college could extend the trial.` Put the condition before or after the main clause, with a comma usually following an initial `if`-clause. Make the condition measurable when possible; a vague condition can hide an unsupported leap.

### B2 — concession, passive forms, and nominalization

`Although the sample was small, it provided useful planning information` acknowledges a limit and a separate value. `Despite the small sample, ...` is followed by a noun phrase or `-ing` form, not a finite clause: `Despite having a small sample, ...`. `However` is a linking adverb, not a coordinating conjunction: `The sample was small; however, the result may guide a larger trial.` In careful writing, use punctuation that shows the relationship.

## C1 — attribution and synthesis

In synthesis, group sources by a shared question, attribute claims, and preserve differences. Compare: `Report A measured self-reported satisfaction; Report B recorded attendance.` These measures do not establish the same outcome. Verbs such as `argues`, `reports`, `finds`, and `suggests` are not interchangeable: select one that accurately describes the source and strength of evidence.

Nominalization converts a process or quality into a noun (`decide` → `decision`, `efficient` → `efficiency`). It can support concise formal writing, but too many abstract nouns can obscure who acts: prefer `The team decided to revise the plan` when the actor matters over `A revision decision was made`.

## C2 — precision, register, and revision

Advanced control includes choosing wording for audience and purpose, not simply making sentences longer. Distinguish an observed result from an interpretation and a recommendation. `The data show a rise in response times` reports an observation; `this may indicate a capacity problem` marks an interpretation; `the team should test additional staffing` is a recommendation.

Keep commitment strength stable when editing: changing `may` to `will` strengthens a claim; changing `must` to `could` weakens an obligation. Revise for clarity, but retain dates, conditions, scope, and uncertainty.

**Practice:** Revise for brevity without changing certainty: `The pilot may improve service, although the available evidence is limited.` **Sample key:** `The pilot may improve service, but evidence remains limited.` Accept other versions retaining both possibility and limitation.

### C1 — attribution, stance, and synthesis

#### Report the source's move accurately

Choose a reporting verb that describes what the source does, not what the writer wishes it had shown. `Reports` introduces information presented by a source; `argues` presents a reasoned position; `finds` commonly reports a study result; `suggests` can introduce a tentative inference or a proposal, so context matters. These verbs are not a fixed scale of certainty. Attribution keeps source content distinct from the writer's interpretation:

- `The survey reports that 68% preferred recorded lectures.`
- `The interviews suggest that tutors value live discussion for identifying confusion.`
- `Together, the sources do not establish which format improves learning outcomes.`

Do not turn a preference finding into an achievement result. Use `according to` or a named source when the attribution would otherwise be unclear. A reporting verb does not make weak evidence stronger.

#### Calibrate stance and concession

Use a hedge only when it represents a real limit in evidence or interpretation: `The result may indicate...`; `The evidence appears to support...`; `The report does not establish...`. `May` and `might` do not encode a universal numeric probability. A concession acknowledges relevant information without necessarily surrendering the main claim:

- `Although attendance rose, access for online-booking users remains uncertain.`
- `Attendance rose; however, the report does not show whether unmet need fell.`

`Although` introduces a clause; `despite` normally takes a noun phrase or an `-ing` form (`Despite the increase...`; `Despite rising attendance...`). Avoid a concession that hides the exact contrast.

**Practice:** Combine accurately: “The survey recorded a preference for recordings. The interviews described the value of live discussion. Neither measured achievement.”  
**Sample key:** `The survey recorded learners' preference for recorded review, whereas tutor interviews highlighted live discussion; neither source measured achievement.` Accept equivalent wording preserving source, construct, and limitation.

#### Choose agency and information focus

Nominalization (`the review of complaints`) can compress information but may hide the actor. Prefer an active clause when responsibility matters (`The panel reviewed the complaints`). A passive can be appropriate when the actor is unknown, irrelevant, or already clear. Fronting and cleft structures guide focus, but use them only when that contrast matters:

- Neutral: `The review did not measure staff workload.`
- Focused: `What the review did not measure was staff workload.`

The second sentence places focus on the missing measure; it does not make the evidence more conclusive.

**Practice:** Make the actor explicit without changing the claim: `A review of the complaints was undertaken by the panel.`  
**Key:** `The panel reviewed the complaints.` Also acceptable: `The panel conducted a review of the complaints.`

### C2 — exact commitment and interpretation

#### Preserve scope, modality, and conditionality

High-stakes editing must keep actor, action, quantity, time, scope, condition, and modality stable. A proposal is not an approval; preference is not commitment; a conditional plan is not unconditional:

- `The panel requested a cost estimate.` does not mean `The panel rejected the proposal.`
- `The team prefers an overtime cap.` does not mean `The team will impose a cap.`
- `Friday remains possible if the data are complete.` does not mean `Friday is confirmed.`

`Provided that` and `subject to` make conditions salient. State what triggers the action and what happens if the condition fails. `May` can express possibility or permission; context determines which. For a decision record, name the actor and action rather than relying on a vague nominalization.

**Practice:** Revise without changing commitment: `The committee approved a full rollout. The source says: “A limited extension is proposed, provided the next review reports branch-level results.”`  
**Key:** `The source proposes a limited extension, conditional on reporting branch-level results in the next review.` The wording must not retain `approved` or `full rollout`.

#### Distinguish entailment, presupposition, and implicature

An **entailment** follows from the literal meaning in context. A **presupposition** is backgrounded by a construction. An **implicature** is a contextual inference that may be cancelled without contradiction. In `Even the revised plan is costly`, the clause asserts that the revised plan is costly and `even` evokes a scale of alternatives; which expectations are assumed depends on context. These categories require analysis of wording and context; do not label every unstated premise a presupposition.

For interpretation, record (1) exact wording, (2) explicit proposition, (3) inference and cue, and (4) what remains unknown. Compare alternatives rather than treating a plausible implication as fact.

**Practice:** In `The report calls the timetable an improvement, provided that improvement means more sessions delivered`, state the explicit qualification and one tempting but unsupported conclusion.  
**Key:** The evaluation is limited to session count; automatic adoption or lower workload is not established.

#### Use emphasis and parallelism purposefully

A cleft focuses information (`What the review omitted was weekend data`). Parallel syntax can make a genuine contrast audible (`We measured requests received, not cases resolved`). Neither structure supplies evidence. Repetition can guide attention, but decorative repetition may obscure the reasoning or over-persuade.

**Practice:** Complete a parallel contrast using the text's actual distinction: `We counted ___, but not ___ .`  
**Sample key:** `We counted the forms processed, but not the cases resolved.` Accept a different accurate output/outcome contrast from the source.

#### Revision fidelity checklist

Before accepting a C2 edit, compare source and revision for:

1. actor and action;
2. date, amount, and population;
3. scope and exception;
4. certainty, obligation, and permission;
5. condition and decision status;
6. attribution and distinction between observation, interpretation, and recommendation.

**Practice:** Explain why `The trial disproved a benefit` is not equivalent to `The trial did not establish a benefit.`  
**Key:** The first claims evidence against benefit; the second says the available evidence did not establish it, leaving the question open.
