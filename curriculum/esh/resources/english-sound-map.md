# English sound map — General American teaching reference

## Model, notation, and purpose

This project uses a **contemporary General American (GA) reference model** for its current synthetic recordings: Piper `en_US/amy/medium` (voice ID `piper-en_US-amy-medium`). This records the user's selected default, not a claim that every clip has passed human quality review or rights clearance. GA is a practical teaching/listening anchor, not a superior accent or a rule for every English speaker. Other English varieties are systematic; a difference from this reference is not automatically an error. At higher levels, learners should meet and understand a range of voices and accents.

The IPA is used to distinguish sounds from letters. A letter is a spelling unit; a phoneme is a sound category that can distinguish words; a phonetic realization is how a speaker produces it in a context. Slashes `/ /` show broad phonemic transcription; brackets `[ ]` can show a more specific phonetic realization. Transcription conventions differ among dictionaries. The examples below are a selected teaching map, not a claim that every GA speaker has one identical inventory.

The Oxford Learner's Dictionaries pronunciation guide was accessed 28 October 2026. It gives symbols and examples for mainstream British and General/Network American pronunciations, explains that broad symbols stand for sound categories rather than single fixed realizations, and describes allophones, weak forms, and syllabic consonants. The International Phonetic Association's official chart was accessed the same day for symbol categories. See [`REFERENCES.md`](../REFERENCES.md).

## Syllables and word stress

A syllable is organized around a vowel nucleus; syllabic /n/ or /l/ can function as the nucleus in words such as `button` or `little` for many speakers. Count spoken beats rather than written vowel letters: `teacher` has two syllables, while `hour` may be one or two depending on speaker and context.

The mark `/ˈ/` precedes primary stress and `/ˌ/` secondary stress in broad IPA. Stress can distinguish word class or meaning:

- `PREsent` (noun/adjective) versus `preSENT` (verb): `/ˈprɛzənt/` vs `/prɪˈzɛnt/` in a common GA transcription.
- `REcord` (noun) versus `reCORD` (verb): stress and vowel quality differ in common GA forms.

These are patterns, not a rule that stress alone always distinguishes noun and verb. Learners can clap or tap syllables, mark the stressed syllable, then listen and repeat. Avoid inserting equal stress on every syllable; preserve the vowel quality and duration of the stressed syllable without exaggerating it.

## Sentence stress, rhythm, and intonation

In many English utterances, content words carry more prominence than predictable grammatical words, but speakers can stress a function word to correct or contrast. The nucleus (main pitch prominence) often signals the information focus:

- `I sent the **FORM** yesterday.` (which item?)
- `I sent the form **YESTERDAY**.` (when?)
- `I **SENT** the form yesterday.` (did I send it or only prepare it?)

Intonation interacts with grammar, information structure, discourse, and speaker intent. A rising contour can occur in yes/no questions, continuation, uncertainty, or many other contexts; falling contours are not a universal marker of confidence or finality. Never infer emotion or attitude from pitch alone without context.

Use `/` for a short phrase boundary in learning scripts and `//` for a longer boundary. Pauses can make structure easier to process but do not replace grammatical cues. At advanced levels, compare plausible focus choices and explain their effect rather than memorizing “one sentence = one tune.”

## Weak forms, connected speech, and reduction

Function words often have a strong form under contrast and a reduced form when unstressed. Examples in common GA speech include `to` `/tə/`, `and` `/ən/` or `/n/`, `can` `/kən/` in an unstressed position, and `of` `/əv/`. The strong forms can return under emphasis or at phrase boundaries. Spelling remains unchanged.

Connected speech includes:

- **linking:** a final consonant may connect smoothly to a following vowel (`pick it up`); GA /r/ is normally pronounced where written in words such as `far away`;
- **glide transitions:** `/j/` or `/w/` may be heard between adjacent vowels in some sequences;
- **assimilation/coalescence:** sounds may become more like a neighbor, as in common pronunciations of `did you` near `/dɪdʒu/`;
- **elision:** a sound may be weakened or omitted in a cluster in some speech styles;
- **flapping:** /t/ and /d/ can be realized as a tap `[ɾ]` in many GA contexts (`water`, `rider`), while `writer/rider` may sound similar for some speakers. This is a context-sensitive realization, not a new spelling or a pronunciation error.

Reduction is variable by speaker, speed, emphasis, and situation. Teach the careful form first, then recognition of common reduced forms; do not require learners to imitate every reduction to be understood.

## Consonant clusters and intelligibility

English permits clusters at word edges (`street` /str-/, `helped` /-lpt/, `next` /-kst/). Learners may insert a vowel or omit a consonant. Teach the cluster in a meaningful word, then contrast it with a new word or phrase. Examples: `play`, `stay`, `spray`; `asks`, `asked`; `text`, `next`. Do not assume every speaker releases every final stop audibly.

When communication breaks down, the priority is repair: repeat at a clear pace, use a different wording, spell a name, or ask which sound/word the listener heard. Accent imitation is not the goal.

## Level progression

| Stage | First presentation and retrieval | Listening target | Production target |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pre-A0 | letter names vs. speech sounds; selected initial consonants; taught CVC vowels | distinguish a name from a word-initial sound; map a heard CVC item to a picture/word | produce a target initial sound and blend a taught short word; no IPA recital |
| A0 / Pre-A1 | retrieve letters/CVC; contrast selected short vowels and common words | hear `ship/sheep`, `live/leave` and familiar personal/location phrases | say practiced short words and a clear personal sentence |
| A1 | contrast meaning-changing consonants; stress in dates/places | retrieve key information in short directions/messages | keep key syllable and word stress intelligible |
| A2 | contrast vowels and consonant clusters; phrase-level linking | follow connected directions and ask for repetition | deliver a short direction/request with clear word groups |
| B1 | retrieve segmentals; sentence rhythm, correction, and contrast | follow qualified reports and hear a correction/repair | use phrase stress to make the correction clear; repair misunderstanding |
| B2 | reduction, focus, and information structure | preserve negation, contrast, and commitment in connected speech | choose focus and pace for an argument, without overstatement |
| C1 | advanced retrieval of stress/intonation and accent exposure | distinguish explicit claim, limit, and context-sensitive stance | use prominence and pauses to guide an audience; explain alternative readings |
| C2 | fine control of focus, scope, rhythm, and pragmatic ambiguity | infer the contrast intended in context while considering other readings | adapt delivery precisely while preserving evidence, qualification, and intent |
| Post-C2 | specialist speaking/mediation transfer | understand professional phrase groups and stated conditions | deliver a professional recommendation with controlled phrasing; no accent imitation requirement |
