# B1–B2 unit learning pack

This pack supplements, rather than replaces, the lessons in `levels/b1.md` and `levels/b2.md`. It adds original, unit-specific input and transfer practice where the level files previously relied on short examples or broad prompts. It does not claim that a single pack validates a CEFR level. Listening scripts remain printed teacher-readable material; selected indexed scripts have supplemental experimental Amy recordings, with no automated pronunciation scores or validated assessment status.

## How to use the pack

For each unit, teach the linked lessons in the level file, then use this unit cycle: retrieve the key language; read the passage and answer the keyed questions; listen to the script twice without seeing it; perform the role task with changed details; produce the writing task; and finish with the unit check in `b1-b2-assessment.md`. First listen for gist, then replay for details. Keep the script covered while listening. For open speaking, writing, and mediation, use the stated success criteria and the shared rubrics in `assessment-rubrics.md`. A successful lesson attempt is practice evidence, not mastery; recheck with new content after a delay.

### Progression from A2 into B1 and onward to B2

| A2 foundation | B1 extension | B2 extension |
|---|---|---|
| Narrate a finished event and state a simple reason | Organize a multi-stage experience; use background and earlier-past forms only when the time relation matters | Select evidence, qualify interpretation, and address an alternative account |
| Write a practical message and connected short account | Develop a paragraph with reasons, examples, cohesion, and a conclusion | Sustain an evidence-based essay or recommendation with a counterargument and audience-aware register |
| Ask for practical information, clarify, and negotiate a plan | Compare options, explain consequences, and summarize a group decision | Challenge the strength of evidence, respond to objections, and state limits and conditions |
| Understand a short teacher-read familiar script | Identify the point, sequence, and key details in a longer familiar-topic script | Track competing positions, qualification, and evidence limits in a denser script |
| Use past/present forms and basic future plans | Extend conditionals, relative clauses, passive forms, and reported information in connected contexts | Use concessive clauses, passive/nominal forms, and hedging to calibrate claims and formal recommendations |

Do not treat these as rigid grammar gates: teach a form when it supports the communication task, and accept other standard English forms when the context permits them.

## B1 Unit 1 — Experiences and narratives

**Links:** B1.U01.L01–L03. **Outcome:** recount an experience in a clear time sequence, distinguish a background action from a completed event, and reflect on its result.

**Language focus:** Past simple moves the events forward; past continuous gives a background; past perfect marks an earlier event only when needed. `I was waiting when the bus arrived.` `The bus had already left, so I called a friend.` Avoid using past perfect for every event.

## B1 Unit 2 — Opinions and reasons

**Links:** B1.U02.L01–L03. **Outcome:** state an opinion, support it with reasons and an example, and acknowledge a reasonable opposing view.

**Language focus:** Separate the claim from its support. Use `because` for a reason, `for example` for an illustration, and `although/however` for contrast. Do not use `therefore` unless the conclusion follows from the reason.

**Vocabulary in context:** `benefit`, `drawback`, `take part`, `make a difference`, `a practical example`, `from my point of view`. `Benefit` and `drawback` name effects, not evidence by themselves.

**Reading:** “Some schools have introduced a short daily reading period. Teachers say it gives learners time to read without a test. A few students prefer to use that time for homework, especially before exams. One school asked classes to choose their own books and reported that more students finished a book that term. The report did not compare the classes with another school, so it cannot show that the reading period alone caused the change.”

## B1 Unit 3 — Problem solving and advice

**Links:** B1.U03.L01–L03 and `B1.W03.L02` in `integrated-skill-labs.md`. **Outcome:** explain a problem and cause, compare feasible responses, and make a recommendation that names a trade-off.

## B1 Unit 5 — Society and public issues

**Links:** B1.U05.L01–L03. **Outcome:** describe benefits and drawbacks, compare positions, and mediate a group recommendation without changing its meaning.

**Language focus:** `Although` introduces a concession within a clause; `however` connects contrasting statements with appropriate punctuation. `On the one hand/on the other hand` signals two perspectives; use a final sentence to state the judgment.

## B2 Unit 1 — Debate and argumentative structure

**Links:** B2.U01.L01–L03 and `B2.W01.L02` in `integrated-skill-labs.md`. **Outcome:** distinguish claim, evidence, and inference; evaluate a limitation; and answer a counterargument fairly.

**Language focus:** Use a hedge that matches the support: `suggests` for an indication, `is consistent with` for compatibility, `does not establish` for an unsupported conclusion. A counterargument should address a serious opposing point, not a weak caricature.

**Vocabulary in context:** `sample`, `comparison group`, `self-reported`, `outcome`, `limitation`, `inference`, `counterargument`. Distinguish an observed outcome from a proposed explanation.

**Reading:** “A college trialled a four-day teaching week in one department. Attendance rose slightly, and participating students reported more time for independent study. The trial lasted six weeks and included 42 volunteers. It did not compare students with a similar group on the usual timetable. The results are promising, but they do not establish that the schedule would work across the college.”

## B2 Unit 2 — Media and persuasion

**Links:** B2.U02.L01–L03. **Outcome:** analyze framing and audience appeal, distinguish selective presentation from evidence, and respond in a formal discussion.

**Language focus:** Attribute interpretations: `The campaign frames the change as...`; `The text emphasizes...`; `It omits...`. Calling a text biased requires evidence of selection, language, or missing context; disagreement alone is not evidence of bias.

## B2 Unit 5 — Health, science, and technology

**Links:** B2.U05.L01–L03. **Outcome:** interpret a limited study accurately, explain uncertainty, and recommend a proportionate next step without giving personal medical advice.

**Language focus:** Distinguish correlation, self-report, and causal evidence. Prefer `participants reported`, `the study found an association`, or `the result may indicate`; do not use `caused` unless the study design supports causality. This unit practices language, not diagnosis or treatment.

**Vocabulary in context:** `participant`, `self-report`, `comparison group`, `adverse effect`, `association`, `follow-up`, `preliminary finding`.

**Reading:** “A six-week app pilot asked 80 adult volunteers to record daily screen breaks and rate their concentration. Participants who used the reminders more often also reported better concentration. The study had no comparison group and did not verify app use independently. The authors recommend a larger trial before drawing conclusions about effectiveness.”

**Writing:** Write 180–220 words advising whether to run a larger trial. State the finding, uncertainty, one risk/ethical safeguard, and a proportionate next step.

## B2 Unit 6 — Culture, identity, and global issues

**Links:** B2.U06.L01–L03. **Outcome:** express a nuanced position, support it without generalizing about groups, and summarize disagreement fairly.

**Language focus:** Use quantifiers and attribution (`some participants`, `in the interviews`, `several respondents`) rather than universal claims. Use concession to acknowledge a valid concern, not to dismiss it. Distinguish an individual account from evidence about a whole population.

**Vocabulary in context:** `belonging`, `perspective`, `stereotype`, `community`, `adapt`, `shared practice`, `representative`. Avoid using identity labels as explanations without evidence.

**Reading:** “A youth exchange programme paired students from two towns. Participants described learning new ways to organize group work, while several said the first meetings felt uncomfortable because routines differed. The organizer’s summary included comments from 20 volunteers but did not ask students who declined to participate. The report recommends a longer orientation and a follow-up survey.”

**Speaking/mediation:** Discuss one fictional exchange-program change. Summarize the group’s agreement and disagreement without assigning views to entire communities. **Success:** specific attribution, one concession, accurate neutral summary.

**Writing:** Write 220–260 words responding to a global or cultural issue. Support a clear position, acknowledge a credible concern, avoid sweeping group claims, and conclude with a practical implication.

**Repair:** circle universal quantifiers (`all`, `always`, `never`); replace unsupported ones with a qualified statement and identify the evidence needed.

## Unit-cycle evidence and progression record

For each unit, record separately:

1. **Exposure:** the learner encountered the lesson and input.
2. **Practice:** the learner attempted the guided and independent tasks.
3. **Performance evidence:** the learner completed the linked unit check and at least one open transfer task.
4. **Mastery decision:** a teacher applied the published criterion to new items and recorded the result.

Do not mark mastery from exposure or completion alone. Unit checks and local thresholds are in `b1-b2-assessment.md`; the coverage matrix describes curriculum evidence, not learner results.
