# C1–C2 advanced learning pack

This original pack develops the six existing unit themes in `levels/c1.md` and `levels/c2.md` into sequenced teaching, practice, and transfer. It is not a CEFR certificate or a detailed official descriptor crosswalk. The Council of Europe pages could not be text-verified in this environment; see `REFERENCES.md`. The local outcomes below are author-designed and should be reviewed by advanced-level ELT specialists.

## Assessment and media boundary

The reading inputs below are written texts. Each listening input remains a separate source script; selected scripts now have supplemental experimental Amy recordings linked beside the activity. Do not display a listening script during its listening task. A generated clip is not human-validated or, by itself, evidence of an independently delivered listening test. Use the paired questions only after listening. No pronunciation score is implied. Open speaking and writing tasks accept multiple defensible responses, scored with the criteria in `assessment-rubrics.md` and the level-specific rubric in `c1-c2-assessment.md`.

## B2 → C1 → C2 progression

| Focus | B2 foundation | C1 development | C2 extension |
|---|---|---|---|
| Argument | Organize a supported position and answer a counterargument | Track the architecture and limits of a complex argument; synthesize sources | Evaluate competing premises and implications; reformulate the argument for different audiences without loss of force or qualification |
| Inference | Identify a stated stance and accessible implication | Infer stance, purpose, and implied meaning from multiple textual cues | Resolve ambiguity among plausible readings, explain confidence and alternatives, and adapt interpretation to context |
| Sources | Distinguish claim, evidence, and limitation | Integrate sources around a question and attribute positions precisely | Reconcile or preserve irreducible disagreement; audit scope, assumptions, and evidential weight |
| Language | Use varied linking and some hedging | Use information structure, modality, reporting, and cohesion flexibly | Select and revise wording at the level of commitment, emphasis, rhythm, register, and pragmatic effect |
| Production | Produce an organized essay and short formal talk | Sustain analytical writing and presentation with audience awareness | Produce controlled, concise or expansive forms on demand; edit for exact intent, genre, and high-stakes consequences |
| Interaction | Respond to objections with reasons | Manage extended disagreement, clarify, and mediate | Maintain shared understanding in ambiguous, fast-moving exchange; restate positions with fine-grained fidelity |

These are course-design distinctions, not quotations from a verified descriptor scale. Complexity is increased through interpretation, synthesis, choice, and transfer—not through rare vocabulary alone.

# C1 unit sequence

## Unit 1 — Long-form reading and argument architecture

**Unit outcome:** analyze a longish argument's claim, sequence, evidence, concession, and conclusion; support an inference with at least two textual cues. **Prerequisite:** B2 claim/evidence/limitation distinctions.

**Lesson sequence:** **C1.U01.L01** annotates claim, reason, evidence, concession, and conclusion; **C1.U01.L02** analyzes cohesion and information flow; **C1.U01.L03** writes a concise analytical summary that preserves scope and uncertainty.

**Language focus:** Distinguish a writer's claim from reported evidence and from the reader's inference. `The report indicates...` attributes; `This may imply...` marks an inference; `The passage establishes...` should be reserved for what follows from the text. A concession (`although`, `while`) may qualify the claim rather than refute it. Track what each pronoun and connective refers to; do not assume `this` points to the nearest noun.

**Reading — The quiet measure:** “When a city reports that a new public service is ‘working’, the word can hide several questions. Has the service reached the people who needed it? Has it reduced the original problem, or merely made the problem less visible? A six-month review of a neighbourhood advice centre found that appointment attendance rose after evening hours were introduced. The review also noted that callers who could not use online booking were still less likely to secure an appointment. The council described the change as a success, citing higher attendance. That description is defensible if attendance is the measure; it is incomplete if access is the aim. A better review would compare who sought help, who received it, and what happened after the meeting. Otherwise, a rise in the number served may be mistaken for a reduction in unmet need.”

## Unit 2 — Academic and professional writing

**Unit outcome:** produce a report, formal response, or research summary whose structure fits purpose and audience, and revise it for precision rather than formality alone. **Prerequisite:** B2 paragraph cohesion and source attribution.

**Lesson sequence:** **C1.U02.L01** plans a report around a decision question; **C1.U02.L02** revises nominalization, agency, and register; **C1.U02.L03** writes and edits a concise evidence-based recommendation.

**Language focus:** Nominalization can compress a process (`the review of complaints`) but may hide who acted; restore agency when accountability matters (`The panel reviewed the complaints`). Fronting can guide emphasis (`Of particular concern is...`) but overuse sounds theatrical. A formal email need not use inflated wording: `I am writing to clarify...` is often more effective than a long ceremonial opening.

**Reading — A report that travels:** “The operations team received repeated reports of confusing return instructions. In response, the team tested a revised one-page guide at two branches. Staff recorded fewer requests for clarification during the test, although they also held brief demonstrations at the counter. The test did not separate the effect of the guide from that of the demonstrations. The team recommends keeping the guide, training staff to introduce it, and checking return-related queries for a further eight weeks. The proposal is deliberately limited: it preserves a low-cost change while gathering evidence before a wider redesign.”

## Unit 3 — Oral argumentation and presentations

**Unit outcome:** deliver a structured five-minute presentation on a complex familiar issue, distinguish evidence from inference, and answer questions by clarifying rather than evading. **Prerequisite:** B2 short presentation and counterargument response.

**Lesson sequence:** **C1.U03.L01** builds a spoken argument map; **C1.U03.L02** uses signposting and emphasis for listeners; **C1.U03.L03** handles challenge questions, clarification, and repair.

**Language focus:** Spoken signposts should orient rather than announce every sentence: `The decision turns on two questions...`; `That evidence supports the first point, not the second.` Use `What I mean by...` to clarify a term and `I would distinguish...` to repair a false equivalence. If a question introduces new information, acknowledge it before answering; do not pretend it was part of the original evidence.

**Reading — The evidence in a presentation:** “A persuasive presentation is not a written essay read aloud. Listeners cannot easily revisit a dense paragraph, so the speaker must make relationships audible: what is known, what remains uncertain, and why the recommendation follows. Signposting helps when it names the relationship (`The limitation matters because...`), but becomes noise when every point is introduced with a stock phrase. A short pause before a qualification can carry more emphasis than another intensifier. The aim is not theatrical confidence; it is a line of reasoning that listeners can reconstruct.”

**Questions/key:** Why is dense written syntax risky in speech? **Listeners cannot easily revisit it.** What is a useful function of a signpost? **Name the relation between points.** What is the text's distinction about confidence? **Confidence of delivery is not a substitute for reconstructable reasoning.**

**Listening script:** “I support retaining the evening clinic, but not on the attendance figure alone. The figure rose after hours changed; we do not yet know whether waiting times fell. If we continue the service for one term and publish both measures, we can make a better decision. A fair question is whether the additional staffing cost is justified. That is why the review should report cost per completed appointment, not just total visits.”

## Unit 4 — Implicit meaning, tone, and rhetorical choices

**Unit outcome:** infer stance, purpose, or subtext from explicit linguistic/contextual cues and name at least one plausible alternative reading where ambiguity remains. **Prerequisite:** B2 source-framing and concession analysis.

**Lesson sequence:** **C1.U04.L01** traces stance through lexical and structural cues; **C1.U04.L02** analyzes irony, understatement, and polite disagreement in context; **C1.U04.L03** paraphrases implied meaning without presenting inference as fact.

**Language focus:** Understatement reduces explicit force (`It was not an ideal outcome` may signal strong dissatisfaction); irony depends on a mismatch between literal wording and context, so do not infer it from wording alone. Modal and reporting choices reveal distance: `apparently`, `seems`, and `is said to` can signal uncertainty or attribution. A reading should cite cues and context; tone is not a free guess.

**Reading — The launch:** “The launch was described as ‘ambitious’ in the opening remarks. The demonstration began twenty minutes late, and the only working screen displayed the draft rather than the final version. The team handled the questions with admirable patience. In its follow-up note, the chair thanked staff for ‘a useful rehearsal’ and confirmed that the public launch would be rescheduled. No reason for the delay was given.”

## Unit 5 — Research and synthesis

**Unit outcome:** integrate two sources around a question, attribute claims accurately, compare what they measure, and write a qualified synthesis. **Prerequisite:** B2 source attribution, paraphrase, and evidence limitations.

**Lesson sequence:** **C1.U05.L01** compares methods and constructs; **C1.U05.L02** is the stable integrated workshop in `integrated-skill-labs.md`; **C1.U05.L03** writes a synthesis and evaluates what the sources cannot establish.

**Language focus:** Synthesis organizes by question, not by source order. Distinguish `both sources address access` from `both show the same effect`. Attribute methods and claims precisely: a survey records reported views; an interview explores explanations; neither automatically establishes causation. Avoid patchwriting: change the conceptual organization, not merely a few words.

**Source A:** “A survey of 410 adult learners at one regional college found that 68% preferred recorded lectures for reviewing complex topics. The survey was voluntary and asked about preference, not test performance.”

**Source B:** “Interviews with 24 tutors at three colleges found that tutors valued live discussion for identifying misunderstandings. Tutors did not compare course results across formats.”

**Reading synthesis questions/key:** What does A measure? **Learner preference for review format.** What does B capture? **Tutor views about identifying misunderstanding in live discussion.** Do the sources show which format improves learning? **No; neither reports a direct comparative learning-outcome measure.** Name one shared theme and one difference. **Both concern learning formats; one captures learner preference for review, the other tutor judgments about discussion.**

**Listening script:** “The two sources are not directly contradictory. Learners value recordings for returning to difficult material; tutors value live discussion for noticing confusion. A course could combine the formats, but that proposal still needs an outcome measure rather than preference alone.”
<!-- ESH-AUDIO:SRC-C25CA87B4340 -->

**Listening check/key:** Why are the sources not contradictory? **They describe different functions and perspectives.** What does the proposed combination require? **Outcome evidence beyond stated preference.**

## Unit 6 — Negotiation and complex interaction

**Unit outcome:** negotiate a decision under competing constraints, ask clarifying questions, reformulate positions fairly, and mediate the outcome with unresolved concerns intact. **Prerequisite:** B2 counterargument, evidence qualification, and interaction repair.

**Lesson sequence:** **C1.U06.L01** listens for interests behind stated positions; **C1.U06.L02** negotiates options and conditions; **C1.U06.L03** delivers a neutral, audience-specific mediation.

**Language focus:** Separate positions (`We need Friday`) from interests (`We need enough time to check the data`). Use conditional offers (`We could agree to Friday if...`) and explicit confirmation (`So the decision is..., while the unresolved issue is...`). Do not report consensus if a participant only agreed to continue discussion.

**Reading — A shared deadline:** “The research group wants to publish a summary before the conference. The communications team needs time to verify the figures, while the researchers want the uncertainty section preserved rather than shortened. Both teams agree that a one-page version is useful. They have not agreed whether the full methods note should accompany it. The chair proposes a concise public summary linked to a fuller methods note, with one day reserved for a final check.”

# C2 unit sequence

## Unit 1 — Precision, nuance, and editorial choice

**Unit outcome:** select and justify wording that preserves scope, force, and pragmatic effect; revise a passage without changing its factual commitments. **C1 → C2 extension:** move from identifying register/stance to making controlled editorial decisions and explaining their consequences.

**Lesson sequence:** **C2.U01.L01** compares near-synonyms in context; **C2.U01.L02** edits for commitment, focus, and rhythm; **C2.U01.L03** produces two audience-specific versions and justifies what changed.

**Language focus:** `assert` presents a claim firmly; `suggest` can indicate or propose, with context deciding which; `concede` acknowledges a point, often reluctantly or strategically; `acknowledge` is more neutral. `Significant` may mean important or statistically significant—do not imply the technical meaning without evidence. Editing must preserve dates, quantities, conditions, and modality.

**Reading — One sentence, three commitments:** “The review found that delays fell during the pilot. It did not establish that the revised process caused the decline. The committee recommends retaining the process provisionally while collecting another quarter of data.” Changing `found` to `proved` overstates the evidence; changing `provisionally` to `permanently` changes the decision. Replacing `did not establish` with `disproved` reverses the epistemic claim.

## Unit 2 — Dense-text interpretation and competing readings

**Unit outcome:** reconstruct a dense argument's assumptions, scope, rhetorical framing, and alternative interpretations, ranking interpretations by textual support. **C1 → C2 extension:** move from supported inference to adjudicating competing plausible inferences and explaining limits.

**Lesson sequence:** **C2.U02.L01** maps claims and hidden premises; **C2.U02.L02** tests implication and alternative readings; **C2.U02.L03** writes an evidence-based interpretive commentary.

**Language focus:** An entailment follows from wording; a presupposition is backgrounded by a construction; an implicature is context-dependent and defeasible. In `Even the revised plan is costly`, the sentence asserts that the revised plan is costly and `even` evokes a scale of alternatives; which expectations the speaker assumes depends on context. Label the kind of inference and do not call every unstated assumption a presupposition.

**Reading — A measured endorsement:** “The revised timetable is an improvement, provided that ‘improvement’ is measured by the number of sessions delivered. Staff absences fell during the trial, though the report does not say whether total workload changed. The timetable is therefore ready for wider discussion, not yet for automatic adoption.”

## Unit 3 — Public speaking and influence

**Unit outcome:** deliver a sustained address with controlled emphasis, adapt in response to challenge, and distinguish persuasive force from evidential support. **C1 → C2 extension:** C1 structures a clear presentation; C2 adapts its rhetorical choices under pressure without distorting evidence.

**Lesson sequence:** **C2.U03.L01** shapes opening, progression, and close for a specified audience; **C2.U03.L02** uses contrast, parallelism, and controlled repetition; **C2.U03.L03** answers adversarial questions while preserving accurate qualification.

**Language focus:** Parallel structure can sharpen a contrast (`not faster at any cost, but faster with safeguards`). Repetition is effective when it marks a real progression; decorative repetition can sound manipulative. A rhetorical question may invite reflection but is not evidence. Use stress and pause to signal contrast; written scripts cannot validate delivery.

**Reading — The address:** “We have counted the forms processed, but not the cases resolved. We have measured the time saved at the front desk, but not the time transferred to callers. The next stage should not abandon the pilot; neither should it declare success. It should measure the whole journey, publish the limits, and return with a decision the public can inspect.”

## Unit 4 — Professional communication and negotiation

**Unit outcome:** negotiate a conditional decision with multiple stakeholders, distinguish commitment from preference, and create an accurate decision record under uncertainty. **C1 → C2 extension:** C1 reaches a workable compromise; C2 preserves exact obligations, contingencies, risks, and unresolved matters in consequential communication.

**Lesson sequence:** **C2.U04.L01** maps interests, authority, and unknowns; **C2.U04.L02** is the stable integrated workshop in `integrated-skill-labs.md`; **C2.U04.L03** writes and verifies a concise decision record and briefing.

**Language focus:** `subject to` introduces a condition; `provided that` can introduce a necessary condition; `we intend to` is not identical to `we commit to`; `may` can mark possibility or permission. In high-stakes writing, specify actor, action, trigger, deadline, and exception. Avoid implying legal effect unless reviewed by a qualified professional.

**Reading — The service pilot:** “Response times increased during two peak periods in a four-week sample; weekend shifts were not included. Operations supports a four-week staffing pilot if weekend data are collected. Finance prefers an overtime cap. The service lead will not support a cap that leaves the busiest shift uncovered. No estimate of customer-satisfaction impact is available. The decision requested today is whether to authorize a measured pilot, not whether to adopt a permanent staffing model.”

## Unit 5 — High-level writing and editing

**Unit outcome:** compose and revise a long-form analytical text for a specified genre, improving argument, cohesion, and precision while retaining source scope. **C1 → C2 extension:** C1 produces structured analysis; C2 revises at multiple levels—claim architecture, information order, rhythm, register, and exact commitment.

**Lesson sequence:** **C2.U05.L01** audits a draft's claim chain; **C2.U05.L02** revises for information focus and sentence rhythm; **C2.U05.L03** edits for concision, consistency, and preservation of nuance.

**Language focus:** A cleft (`What the review did not measure was...`) directs focus; use it when the contrast matters, not as decoration. A short sentence can sharpen a conclusion, while a longer sentence can hold qualifications together. Replacing a noun with a verb often restores agency. A revision is successful only if it improves the intended effect without changing scope or certainty.

**Reading — Editorial alternatives:** “The pilot reduced reported waiting time in two clinics. Because the clinics volunteered and changed their booking system at the same time, the result cannot be attributed to staffing alone. A full rollout is premature. A further trial should include clinics with different booking systems and publish both waiting-time and staffing measures.” The final two sentences are short because they present a boundary and a next step, not because short sentences are always superior.

## Unit 6 — Advanced interaction and mediation

**Unit outcome:** mediate disagreement with precise attribution, respond flexibly to ambiguity, and adapt the same content to specialist and non-specialist audiences without collapsing distinctions. **C1 → C2 extension:** C1 summarizes and negotiates; C2 manages uncertainty, competing interpretations, audience shifts, and face-sensitive repair in real time.

**Lesson sequence:** **C2.U06.L01** tracks explicit versus inferred commitments; **C2.U06.L02** repairs misunderstanding and tests paraphrase; **C2.U06.L03** creates two audience-specific mediations and explains trade-offs.

**Language focus:** `So you are saying...` tests a paraphrase; `Have I understood correctly that...` invites correction. A neutral summary can preserve disagreement without false balance: attribute positions and show whether evidence differs. Politeness does not require obscuring a substantive disagreement. Mark uncertainty rather than smoothing it away.

**Reading — Two accounts of the same decision:** “The project lead said the deadline ‘remains achievable’ if data arrive by Tuesday. The analyst said the data are incomplete and that a Thursday review is the earliest defensible date. The chair recorded a Friday submission as the current plan, subject to a completeness check. None of the speakers said that the analysis itself was complete.”
